Associations between fruit, vegetable and fish and epigenetic markers of ageing and protein EpiScores in Generation Scotland

Sarah Gregory, Jure Mur, Oliver M. Shannon, Hannah M. Smith, Riccardo E. Marioni, Makis Tzioras, Graciela Muniz-Terrera

GeroScience · 26 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1007/s11357-026-02654-5

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

In several thousand Scottish adults, how much fruit, vegetables and fish people ate was linked to several DNA methylation markers of biological ageing, hinting that diet leaves traces on the epigenome.

Why it matters

Eating more fruit, vegetables and fish has long been tied to lower risk of chronic diseases such as heart disease and to longer life. Why this happens is less clear. One idea is that diet changes DNA methylation, the chemical tags on DNA that help control which genes are active and whose pattern shifts with age. If certain foods are linked to younger-looking methylation patterns, that would offer one possible route from the dinner plate to healthier ageing, and would give researchers measurable markers to track in diet studies.

What they did

The researchers used Generation Scotland, a large population study with dietary information and blood DNA methylation data. Diet data covered how often and how much people ate fresh fruit (8,648 people), vegetables (8,686 people) and fish (5,070 people). They tested links between each of these foods and several methylation-based markers: GrimAge and PhenoAge, two epigenetic clocks tied to health and mortality risk; DNAm telomere length, a methylation estimate of telomere length; a methylation marker of the inflammatory protein IL6; and EpiScores for 109 proteins, which are methylation-based stand-ins for blood protein levels. They used linear regression and corrected for testing many markers at once. The study was cross-sectional, looking at one point in time.

What they found

After correction for multiple testing, there were several significant associations between the foods and the epigenetic biomarkers. The abstract does not list which foods were linked to which markers, nor the direction or size of the effects, so this summary cannot report them. The authors conclude that their findings add to evidence that eating habits are associated with DNA methylation-derived markers of ageing and health. They call for checking the results in other groups of people and for using data collected over time to explore whether diet actually causes these changes.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, and the abstract gives no specific results, effect sizes or directions, so it is not possible to say here which links were found. Diet was self-reported, which is often inaccurate. The design is cross-sectional and observational: people who eat more fruit, vegetables and fish may also differ in income, exercise, smoking and other habits that could explain the links. The results come from one Scottish population and have not yet been confirmed elsewhere, as the authors note. EpiScores and DNAm telomere length are indirect estimates rather than direct measures of protein levels or telomeres.

Key terms

DNA methylation
Small chemical tags on DNA that help switch genes on or off and change with age.
GrimAge
An epigenetic clock built to predict lifespan and health risk.
PhenoAge
An epigenetic clock trained on blood markers of health and mortality.
DNAm telomere length
An estimate of telomere length made from DNA methylation rather than measured directly.
EpiScore
A methylation-based score that stands in for the blood level of a particular protein.
Cross-sectional study
A study that measures people at a single point in time.
