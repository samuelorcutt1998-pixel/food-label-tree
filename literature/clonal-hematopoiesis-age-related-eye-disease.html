Clonal hematopoiesis and the risk of major age-related eye diseases

Ruijie Xie, Robert P. Finger, Davide Garzone, Lei Peng, Hermann Brenner, Ben Schöttker

GeroScience · 19 Nov 2026 · Research

In over 436,000 UK Biobank adults, carrying age-related blood-cell mutations (CHIP) was linked to a higher risk of cataract, macular degeneration and diabetic eye disease, though most effects were small.

Why it matters

As we age, blood stem cells in the bone marrow pick up random DNA changes. Occasionally one of these somatic mutations gives a stem cell a growth edge, and its descendants expand into a sizeable share of the blood. When this happens without any blood cancer it is called clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, or CHIP. CHIP becomes more common with age and is one of the clearest, measurable examples of how mutations accumulating in our own tissues can shape health. It has already been tied to heart disease and other conditions, probably in part because the mutated immune cells push the body toward chronic inflammation. Whether it also matters for the eye's common age-related diseases, such as cataract, glaucoma and macular degeneration, has been uncertain. This study asks that question in a very large population.

What they did

The team used UK Biobank, a cohort of volunteers who enrolled between 2006 and 2010 and whose health records were followed until March 2022. Whole-exome sequencing of blood DNA was used to spot CHIP, defined as known driver mutations present in at least 2% of the sequenced DNA copies (the variant allele fraction). They then followed people for new diagnoses of cataract, glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and diabetic retinopathy, the last analysed only in people with diabetes. Statistical models adjusted for other factors and estimated hazard ratios, which describe how much higher the rate of a new diagnosis was in people with CHIP. They also looked at which gene carried the mutation, how big the clone was, and whether blood counts and inflammation markers explained the links.

What they found

Of 436,469 participants, with an average age of about 56, 14,110 (3.2%) had CHIP. Over a median of 14.4 years, people with CHIP had a modestly higher risk of cataract (about 8% higher) and AMD (about 12% higher), and a clearly higher risk of diabetic retinopathy among people with diabetes (about 48% higher). For glaucoma overall the link was not statistically significant, but it was significant in people without high blood pressure. The size of the association differed by clone size and by which driver gene was mutated. Blood and inflammation markers explained less than 5% of the links, so the measured inflammation does not seem to be the main route.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, because the full text is paywalled. This is an observational study, so it shows associations, not that CHIP causes eye disease. Apart from diabetic retinopathy the effects were small, as the authors themselves note. UK Biobank volunteers tend to be healthier and are mostly of European ancestry, and the eye diagnoses came from health records rather than eye examinations. CHIP was measured once, at enrolment, so changes in clones over time were not captured.

Key terms

CHIP
Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential: an expanded group of blood cells carrying the same acquired mutation, without a blood cancer.
Somatic mutation
A DNA change that arises in a body cell during life rather than being inherited.
Variant allele fraction
The share of DNA reads that carry a given mutation; a rough measure of how big a clone is.
Hazard ratio
How much higher (or lower) the rate of a new event is in one group compared with another over follow-up.
Age-related macular degeneration
Damage to the central retina that blurs central vision, mostly in older adults.
