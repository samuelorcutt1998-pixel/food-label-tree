PARP-CD38-Driven NAD+ Loss in Immune Aging: An Emerging Mechanistic Framework

Ali U, Tan R, Ding S, Ji S

Aging and Disease · 17 Oct 2026 · Review

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.14336/ad.2026.0890

Open access. The full paper is free to read (free copy).

A review proposing how the DNA-repair enzyme PARP and the enzyme CD38 together drain the cell fuel NAD+ in aging immune cells, and why that matters for anti-aging drugs.

Why it matters

As the immune system ages it becomes both weaker and more inflamed, a state often called inflammaging. One idea links this to NAD+, a molecule cells need for energy and for many repair jobs. A family of enzymes called PARPs uses up NAD+ when they respond to DNA damage, especially PARP1, the main enzyme that flags broken DNA for repair. Another enzyme, CD38, also breaks down NAD+ and becomes more common in inflamed, aged tissues. NAD+ boosting supplements and PARP-blocking drugs are both being explored, so understanding how DNA-damage responses, NAD+ and immune aging fit together matters for anyone weighing these approaches.

What they did

This is a review article, so the authors did not run new experiments. According to the abstract, they gathered existing research and proposed a framework they call a context-dependent PARP-CD38-NAD+-inflammaging hierarchy. They looked beyond PARP1 at other members of the family and at how NAD+ is kept in separate pools inside the cell, each with its own enzymes for making and moving it.

What they found

The authors note that PARP1 accounts for about 85 to 90 percent of PARP activity in the nucleus and is the main consumer of NAD+ during acute DNA damage, while CD38 is the main NAD+-destroying enzyme and rises in inflamed, aged tissue. They point out that NAD+ is held in distinct compartments, such as the cytosol and the mitochondria, with different regulators. They highlight recent human data that challenge the idea that NAD+ declines everywhere with age: one study found that an 85 percent drop in muscle NAD+ did not impair function, and whole-blood NAD+ did not change with age. From this they argue that NAD+ decline is specific to tissues and compartments, and that blood NAD+ is not a reliable marker. They list other PARPs tied to aging of particular tissues in models, including PARP2 in mouse heart muscle cells, PARP3 in the gut, PARP16 in blood vessels and PARP9 and PARP14 in Alzheimer's models, while noting their roles in human immune aging are not yet measured. PARP inhibitors and NAD+ precursors show promise, they write, but carry risks including genomic instability, blood-cell toxicity, promoting senescence and inconsistent benefit.

Limits

As a review and proposed framework, it does not test its own hypothesis, and the authors say key questions remain open: whether PARP activity in blood immune cells predicts immune aging, how sex differences play in, and whether partly blocking PARP could save NAD+ without harming DNA repair. Several of the cited links come from animal or disease models rather than people. This synopsis was written from the abstract only, although the full review is free to read.

Key terms

NAD+
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, a molecule cells use for energy metabolism and as fuel for some repair and signalling enzymes.
PARP1
Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1, an enzyme that detects DNA breaks and helps start repair, using up NAD+ as it works.
CD38
An enzyme on immune and other cells that breaks down NAD+; it becomes more abundant in inflamed, aging tissue.
Inflammaging
The chronic, low-grade inflammation that tends to build up with age.
PARP inhibitor
A drug that blocks PARP enzymes; used in some cancers and being studied for its effects on NAD+.
NAD+ precursor
A supplement or compound, such as nicotinamide riboside, that the body can turn into NAD+.
