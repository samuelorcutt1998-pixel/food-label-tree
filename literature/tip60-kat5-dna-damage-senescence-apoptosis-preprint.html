TIP60/KAT5-mediated regulation of apoptotic sensitivity during DNA damage-induced senescence

Shiksha Dutta, Nicolas Malaquin, Erwan Goy, Audrey Carrier- Leclerc, Guillaume Cardin, Francis Rodier

bioRxiv (preprint) · 16 Nov 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.64898/2026.11.12.766234

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Preprint: the level of one chromatin enzyme, TIP60, set how DNA-damaged senescent cells repair breaks, how much inflammation they secrete and whether a senolytic drug can kill them.

Why it matters

When a cell suffers heavy DNA damage it usually takes one of two paths: it dies (apoptosis) or it becomes senescent, a permanent arrest in which it stays alive and often releases inflammatory signals known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Senescent cells build up with age and are a target of 'senolytic' drugs designed to kill them selectively. But senescent cells are not all alike, and it is unclear what decides whether a damaged cell dies or becomes senescent, or how inflammatory and how drug-sensitive it will be. Proteins that modify chromatin, the packaging of DNA, are good candidates because they affect both DNA break repair and which genes are switched on.

What they did

The researchers studied normal human fibroblasts (connective-tissue cells) exposed to a single 10 Gy dose of ionising radiation, which causes DNA double-strand breaks and drives the cells into senescence. They altered the levels of TIP60 (also called KAT5), an enzyme that adds acetyl groups to proteins and helps repair DNA breaks. They then measured proliferation arrest, the clearing of DNA damage markers 53BP1 and γH2AX, SASP factors at the transcript and protein level, p53 activity, the anti-death protein MCL-1, and sensitivity to the senolytic drug ABT-263 (navitoclax).

What they found

TIP60 was not required for the cells to stop dividing after radiation, but its level controlled several other features. The resolution of 53BP1 and γH2AX damage foci, and the strength of the SASP, depended on how much TIP60 the cells had. Cells with low TIP60 showed reduced p53 output and high levels of MCL-1, and were completely resistant to ABT-263. Cells with high TIP60 were modestly more sensitive to the drug. The authors conclude that TIP60 sits upstream of where senescent phenotypes diverge, that proliferation arrest and the SASP can be uncoupled by the level of a single chromatin modifier, and that TIP60 could help predict senolytic response. They suggest adding MCL-1 inhibition where TIP60 is low.

Limits

This is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed, so its findings may change. This synopsis is based on the abstract only. The work uses one cell type (normal human fibroblasts) and one form of damage (a single high radiation dose) in culture; senescence in aged tissues builds up over years from many causes and in many cell types. The abstract does not report animal or human data, and the proposal to combine senolytics with MCL-1 inhibitors is a suggestion, not a tested treatment.

Key terms

TIP60 / KAT5
A lysine acetyltransferase enzyme that modifies chromatin and helps repair DNA double-strand breaks.
Cellular senescence
A permanent stop to cell division that often comes with inflammatory secretions.
SASP
The senescence-associated secretory phenotype: the mix of inflammatory factors released by senescent cells.
53BP1 and γH2AX foci
Spots in the nucleus that mark DNA double-strand breaks; they disappear as breaks are repaired.
Senolytic
A drug designed to kill senescent cells selectively; ABT-263 (navitoclax) is one.
MCL-1
A protein that blocks apoptosis (programmed cell death).
