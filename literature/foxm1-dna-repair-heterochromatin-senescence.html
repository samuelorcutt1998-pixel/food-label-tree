FOXM1 Expression Enhances DNA Repair in Aged Cells to Maintain the Peripheral Heterochromatin Barrier Against Senescence Enhancers

Carlos Sousa‐Soares, Maria M. da Silva, José P. Castro, Joana C. Macedo, Elsa Logarinho

Aging Cell · 16 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1111/acel.70844

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Aged cells lose the transcription factor FOXM1, which shuts down DNA repair genes; restoring it cut DNA damage and the epigenetic changes that switch on senescence and inflammation.

Why it matters

DNA damage builds up as we age and is thought to drive several other hallmarks of aging, including erosion of the epigenome (the chemical marks and packaging that control which genes are on), cellular senescence (cells that stop dividing and release inflammatory signals) and chronic low-grade inflammation. Yet there are still no proven ways to protect the genome against aging. One reason is that it is unclear why DNA repair weakens in old cells in the first place. If a single master switch were responsible for turning down many repair genes at once, it could be a target for therapy. This study proposes that the transcription factor FOXM1 is such a switch.

What they did

Working with aged cells, the researchers examined how FOXM1 levels relate to the activity of DNA repair genes and the amount of DNA damage. They then restored FOXM1 activity in aged cells and measured DNA damage, epigenetic changes and senescence. To understand the mechanism, they traced how FOXM1-driven repair affects G9a, an enzyme that places a repressive mark called H3K9me2 on histones, and how that mark is distributed at the nuclear periphery, where tightly packed 'heterochromatin' normally sits. Finally they looked at enhancers, the DNA switches that turn on the AP-1-driven senescence and inflammation program.

What they found

According to the abstract, FOXM1 is repressed during aging, and this accounts for a broad transcriptional shutdown of DNA repair genes and a build-up of DNA damage. Restoring FOXM1 in aged cells reduced DNA damage and the epigenetic alterations that drive senescence. The proposed chain is: FOXM1 switches on DNA repair genes; better repair prevents DNA damage from triggering the breakdown of the G9a enzyme; G9a is therefore kept, and with it the H3K9me2 mark that anchors heterochromatin at the edge of the nucleus. With this anchor maintained, enhancers of the AP-1-driven senescence and inflammation program were inactivated. The authors describe FOXM1 as an 'age-reversal factor' that restores genetic and epigenetic integrity.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only. The article is listed as open access, but its full text was not reviewed for this page, so sample sizes, effect sizes and methods details are not given here. The abstract describes work in aged cells; it does not report effects on lifespan, tissue function or health in whole animals or people, so the 'age-reversal' label refers to cellular markers. FOXM1 is also known as a driver of cell division and is active in many cancers, so boosting it as a therapy would need careful safety testing; the abstract does not address this. Which cell types and how the cells were aged are not specified in the abstract.

Key terms

FOXM1
A transcription factor (gene switch protein) that turns on genes for cell division and, per this study, DNA repair.
DNA repair
The set of cell systems that detect and fix damage to DNA.
Heterochromatin
Tightly packed DNA, often at the nuclear edge, where genes are kept switched off.
H3K9me2
A chemical mark on histone proteins associated with silenced, compact chromatin.
G9a
The enzyme that places the H3K9me2 mark.
Enhancer
A DNA region that boosts the activity of nearby genes when bound by the right proteins.
AP-1
A family of transcription factors that drive inflammatory and senescence gene programs.
