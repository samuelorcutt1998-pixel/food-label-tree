Regenerative Stress Contributes to Higher Clonal Hematopoiesis with Myelodysplastic Features in Sickle Cell Disease Mice

Mengna Chi et al.

Blood · 16 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1182/blood.2026034793

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Sickle cell mice showed premature 'aged' blood stem cell mutations and more clonal hematopoiesis, and forcing extra regeneration raised mutation burden further, linking stem-cell stress to somatic mutation.

Why it matters

Clonal hematopoiesis is a hallmark of an aging blood system: as we get older, blood stem cells pick up somatic mutations (DNA changes acquired during life), and a few mutated stem cells expand to produce a large share of blood cells. It raises the risk of blood cancers and heart disease. A key question is what speeds this up. One idea is that stem cells forced to divide and regenerate a lot accumulate mutations faster. People with sickle cell disease are a natural test case: their red blood cells break down constantly, so the bone marrow is under chronic pressure to make more, and as patients now live longer, a higher risk of blood cancers is emerging, including after gene therapy or stem cell transplants.

What they did

The researchers used humanized sickle cell disease mice and compared them with normal mice matched for age, sex and strain. They monitored blood production in blood and bone marrow repeatedly up to one year of age. They used highly sensitive, error-corrected deep sequencing of 450 genes associated with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and other blood cancers to detect low-level mutations. To test regenerative stress directly, they performed serial hematopoietic stem cell transplants, which force stem cells to rebuild the blood system repeatedly.

What they found

Unlike the control mice, the sickle cell mice showed an 'ageing-like' blood system: they developed myeloid-biased blood production early and their stem and progenitor cells carried a mutational profile characteristic of aged mice and humans. A high proportion developed blood production with dysplastic (abnormal-looking) features resembling MDS and had a higher clonal hematopoiesis burden. Serial transplantation further increased the mutational burden of stem and progenitor cells and led to the emergence or expansion of clones carrying oncogenic driver mutations. The authors conclude that regenerative stress, whether from chronic demand for red cells or from autologous transplant, raises the risk of clonal hematopoiesis and MDS-like changes, and suggest early curative therapy might avoid this risk.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only. Crossref lists a CC BY-NC-ND licence, but the full text was not reviewed for this page, so mouse numbers, mutation counts and effect sizes are not given here. The work is in mice, which age faster and differ from humans in blood biology, and the follow-up ran to one year of age. Sickle cell disease is a special situation, so how far the findings apply to normal human aging is an inference. The suggestion that early curative therapy reduces the risk is a hypothesis, not something tested in this study.

Key terms

Clonal hematopoiesis
Expansion of blood stem cells carrying acquired mutations, common with age and linked to cancer and heart disease.
Somatic mutation
A DNA change acquired during life in body cells, not inherited.
Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)
A group of disorders where the bone marrow makes abnormal blood cells; can progress to leukemia.
Hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPC)
The bone marrow cells that make all blood cells.
Error-corrected deep sequencing
A DNA sequencing method that tags molecules to filter out errors, allowing very rare mutations to be detected.
Myeloid bias
A shift of blood production toward myeloid cells (such as neutrophils), typical of aged blood systems.
