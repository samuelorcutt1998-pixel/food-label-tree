Mutation Accumulation Underpins Evolution of Lifespan Extension by Dietary Restriction

Sara D. Irish, Annabel Kimberley, Simone Immler, Jacob Moorad, Alexei A. Maklakov

Aging Cell · 5 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1111/acel.70808

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

In roundworms, dietary restriction still lengthens life when reproduction is forced back up, pointing to mutation accumulation—not a reproduction–repair trade-off—as the evolutionary driver.

Why it matters

Eating less extends lifespan in many animals, but biologists still disagree about why that trait evolved. The usual story is a trade-off: when food is scarce, the body shifts resources from reproduction into somatic maintenance and DNA or cellular repair, so survival rises. If that trade-off is wrong, then lifespan on rich food may instead erode because selection against late-life harm is weak when fertility is high—an idea tied to mutation accumulation. Sorting those explanations matters for how we read dietary-restriction biology and for whether “spend less on reproduction, buy more repair” is the right framing.

What they did

According to the abstract, the authors worked in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans. They built an experimental setup that uses food odour cues to trick worms under dietary restriction into putting more effort into reproduction, without restoring plentiful food. That let them ask whether raising reproductive effort under restriction cancels the survival benefit. They also built a theoretical model based on Hamilton’s forces of selection to show when lifespan extension under restriction can evolve.

What they found

Dietary restriction improved survival as expected. Forcing higher reproduction under restriction did not erase either the immediate or the longer-term survival gains, which runs against a simple resource-allocation trade-off between fertility and somatic maintenance. The authors argue that much of the fertility drop under restriction is restraint—holding back from reproducing in a poor environment—rather than an obligatory spend of the same resource pool on repair. Their model shows that lifespan extension under restriction can evolve because suppressed fertility itself strengthens selection against mortality in that environment, and that this can happen under a wider set of conditions than models that demand strong physiological or genetic trade-offs. On the flip side, they show how poorer survival on plentiful food can evolve through mutation accumulation when selection against late harm is relaxed. In that framing, long life under scarce food is less a purchased upgrade in repair and more a side effect of how fertility changes the force of selection.

Limits

This synopsis was written from the abstract only, although the paper is open access under a CC BY license. The experiments are in C. elegans with an odour-cue trick to raise reproduction, so how far the result generalizes to mammals or to human caloric restriction is open. The evolutionary model is analytical; it does not by itself map which molecular damage pathways (for example DNA repair versus other maintenance) are the ones selection is acting on.

Key terms

Dietary restriction (DR)
A sustained cut in food intake without malnutrition that often lengthens lifespan in lab animals.
Resource allocation trade-off
The idea that energy or materials spent on reproduction cannot also be spent on keeping the body repaired.
Mutation accumulation
An evolutionary process in which weakly selected harmful variants build up because selection against late-life damage is weak.
Hamilton’s forces of selection
A framework describing how strongly natural selection opposes mortality and fertility loss at each age.
Caenorhabditis elegans
A short-lived nematode worm widely used to study aging genetics and dietary restriction.
Somatic maintenance
The body’s ongoing upkeep of cells and molecules, including damage repair, as opposed to reproduction.
