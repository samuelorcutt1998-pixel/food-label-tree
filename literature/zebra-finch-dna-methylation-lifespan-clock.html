DNA methylation correlates with lifespan and predicts remaining lifespan in the zebra finch

Marianthi Tangili, Per J Palsbøll, Simon Verhulst

GeroScience · 10 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1007/s11357-026-02646-5

Paywalled. The full paper needs a subscription; the abstract is free at the DOI link.

Following 50 zebra finches until natural death, the speed at which each bird's epigenetic age rose, not a single reading, predicted how long it lived.

Why it matters

Epigenetic clocks estimate biological age from methyl tags on DNA, and in people, those who read 'older' than their real age tend to die sooner. A big open question is whether this link between the epigenome and lifespan is a general feature of aging across animals or something peculiar to humans and a few lab mammals. Birds are a useful test because they are distant relatives of mammals and many live long for their body size. This study is also unusual because it followed the same animals until they died of natural causes, which lets the authors ask directly whether DNA methylation predicts how much life an individual has left, rather than relying on population averages.

What they did

The researchers studied 50 captive adult zebra finches, a widely used bird model, and monitored them until their natural deaths. They sequenced whole-genome DNA methylation twice per bird, giving 100 longitudinal methylomes. They looked for genome-wide trends with age and for individual CpG sites (spots on DNA that can carry a methyl tag) whose methylation changed with age. They then built an epigenetic clock to predict chronological age, and compared how far each bird's clock age sat from its real age (epigenetic age acceleration) with its early-life conditions and its lifespan. Because each bird was sampled twice, they could also measure how fast each individual's epigenetic age changed between samples. Finally, they trained a separate 'doom' clock aimed at predicting how long a bird would live after sampling.

What they found

Methylation across the genome fell with age (hypomethylation), and it fell faster in birds that went on to have shorter lives. The authors identified 29 CpG sites whose methylation changed significantly with age. Their clock, based on 119 CpG sites, predicted chronological age with a median absolute deviation of 0.68 years, about 8.2% of the maximum lifespan in the dataset. Females raised in large broods, which are known to live shorter lives, showed more epigenetic age acceleration, consistent with faster biological aging. However, a single measure of age acceleration did not predict lifespan or remaining lifespan. What did predict lifespan was the within-bird rate of change: birds whose epigenetic age rose faster between samples lived shorter lives. The 'doom' clock trained on post-sampling lifespan also successfully predicted remaining lifespan. The authors describe this as the first evidence that DNA methylation offers a window into biological aging in birds.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the article appears to be paywalled and its full text was not used here. The study used 50 captive birds, so the sample is modest and lab conditions differ from life in the wild. Each bird had only two methylation samples, so the 'rate of change' is based on two time points. The abstract does not give the strength of the lifespan predictions or say whether the doom clock was tested on birds outside those used to train it, so how well it would work in new animals is unclear. Results in one bird species may not carry over to other birds or to mammals.

Key terms

DNA methylation
Small chemical tags (methyl groups) on DNA that help switch genes on or off and shift with age.
CpG site
A spot in DNA where a C is followed by a G; the usual place methyl tags sit.
Epigenetic clock
A model that estimates age from methylation at a chosen set of CpG sites.
Epigenetic age acceleration
How much older or younger an individual reads on a clock than its actual age.
Hypomethylation
A loss of methyl tags, here seen across the genome with age.
Longitudinal
Measuring the same individuals more than once over time.
