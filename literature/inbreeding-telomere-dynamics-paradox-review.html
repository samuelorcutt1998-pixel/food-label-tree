Disentangling the paradox of inbreeding and telomere dynamics

Michael L. Pepke, Morten T. Limborg

Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B · 12 Nov 2026 · Review

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1098/rstb.2026.0110

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A cross-species review finds that inbred lab and domestic strains tend to have longer telomeres, yet inbred individuals in wild populations, including people, tend to have shorter ones, and explains why.

Why it matters

Telomeres, the repeated DNA sequences that cap and protect chromosome ends, are widely used as a biomarker of health, aging and fitness. Inbreeding (having closely related parents) is known to lower fitness, but how that happens at the molecular level is hard to study in wild animals. Studies linking inbreeding to telomere length have reported every possible answer: shorter, longer and no difference. Sorting out that contradiction matters for anyone interpreting telomere measurements, in conservation biology and in evolutionary studies of aging.

What they did

This is a review and synthesis. The authors gathered published evidence across eukaryotes (organisms from yeast to humans) on telomere length and inbreeding. They separated two kinds of comparison: studies comparing whole species or strains assumed to differ in inbreeding history because of domestication or captive breeding, and studies relating measured individual inbreeding levels to telomere length within natural populations. The article is part of a themed issue on 'Developmental circuits in ageing'.

What they found

The two kinds of comparison pointed in opposite directions. When species or strains with assumed different inbreeding histories were compared, most studies found longer telomeres with more inbreeding. When individual inbreeding was measured within natural populations, including humans, most studies found shorter telomeres with more inbreeding. In both cases, the authors note there is very little evidence on how large and how reliable these effects are. They argue that the long telomeres of domesticated and laboratory organisms may be a side effect of captive breeding and artificial selection rather than of inbreeding itself. If inbreeding does shorten telomeres in wild populations, that would affect how telomere biomarkers are read in evolutionary and conservation work.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the article is open access. As a review it does not report new data, and the abstract does not describe how studies were found or whether a formal meta-analysis was done. The authors themselves stress how thin the evidence is on effect size and reliability. The proposed explanation for long telomeres in lab strains is an interpretation, not a tested result. The paper is about telomeres as population biomarkers, not about whether telomere length causes aging in any individual.

Key terms

Telomere
Repeated DNA at chromosome ends that protects them and tends to shorten with age.
Inbreeding
Mating between close relatives, which increases shared gene copies.
Inbreeding depression
Reduced health or fitness in offspring of related parents.
Eukaryotes
Organisms whose cells have a nucleus, from yeast to humans.
Artificial selection
Humans breeding animals or strains for chosen traits.
Biomarker
A measurable sign used to indicate health or biological state.
