Recurrent mitotic error and purifying selection shape chromosomal mosaicism across human tissues

David Tang et al.

bioRxiv (preprint) · 2 Nov 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.64898/2026.10.27.760146

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Deep sequencing of 19 healthy tissues from 25 older donors maps where large chromosome errors pile up with age, and shows the body weeds most of them out.

Why it matters

Every time a cell divides it has to copy and split its chromosomes perfectly. Sometimes it does not, and a daughter cell ends up missing a chromosome, carrying an extra one, or losing a large chunk of one. These large changes are called mosaic chromosomal alterations, because only some cells in the body carry them, like tiles in a mosaic. Scientists know a lot about them in cancer and in blood, where they are part of clonal hematopoiesis, the age-related expansion of mutated blood cell families. Much less is known about how common they are in healthy solid organs such as the heart, lung or glands, and how that changes as people age. Because genomic instability is considered one of the hallmarks of aging, a map of these errors across the body helps show where chromosome damage accumulates and whether the body tolerates it.

What they did

According to the abstract, the team used very deep whole-genome sequencing (more than 200-fold coverage) produced by the Somatic Mosaicism across Human Tissues (SMaHT) Network, a large research consortium. The data covered 19 different tissues from 25 donors aged 42 to over 89, for a total of 722 tissue samples. They searched these samples for chromosome changes longer than one million DNA letters, for losses of the sex chromosomes (X and Y), and for copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity, a change where a cell keeps the normal amount of DNA but both copies of a region come from the same parent.

What they found

The researchers found 1,008 large mosaic changes on the non-sex chromosomes, and losses of the sex chromosomes turned up everywhere. Unexpectedly, loss of the Y chromosome in men was highest in the lung, adrenal gland and heart, reaching roughly 5 to 10 percent of cells in elderly donors. Those same tissues tended to show more X loss in women, which the authors read as a gradient in how often cell division goes wrong from tissue to tissue. Changes in whole chromosome number showed tissue-specific patterns suggesting that the body usually selects against cells carrying them. The adrenal gland was an exception, with low levels of such errors almost everywhere. The testis rarely had extra or missing chromosomes, but it often showed copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity, hinting at more frequent swapping between paired chromosomes in sperm stem cells. Repeated X losses in women's tissues favoured one parental copy over the other, which the authors explain through a mix of genetic and epigenetic effects. Overall, they conclude that the chromosome landscape of aging tissues is shaped by genetics and recurring errors, and that most of these errors are kept in check by purifying selection, the removal of less fit cells.

Limits

This is a preprint, so it has not yet been checked by independent peer reviewers, and the findings could change. The study included 25 donors, which is a small number of people even though many tissues were sampled from each, and all donors were middle-aged or older, so it cannot show directly how these changes build up from youth. The abstract describes patterns and the authors' interpretation of selection; it does not report effects on health or disease. This synopsis was written from the abstract only.

Key terms

Mosaic chromosomal alteration
A large gain, loss or rearrangement of chromosome material that is present in only some of a person's cells.
Aneuploidy
Having the wrong number of chromosomes in a cell, usually from an error during cell division.
Loss of Y (or X)
A cell dropping one of its sex chromosomes; loss of Y in blood is a well-known marker that rises with age in men.
Copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity
A change where a cell keeps two copies of a DNA region, but both now come from the same parent.
Purifying selection
The tendency for cells carrying harmful changes to be outcompeted or removed, keeping their numbers low.
SMaHT Network
Somatic Mosaicism across Human Tissues, a research consortium cataloguing DNA changes that arise in the body's cells after conception.
