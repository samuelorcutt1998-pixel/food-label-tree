Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential and risk of intervertebral disc degeneration: a large prospective cohort study

Qijun Wang, Xuan Zhao, Shibao Lu

Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research · 11 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1186/s13018-026-07383-1

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

In about 259,000 UK Biobank adults, carrying age-related blood-cell mutations was linked to a roughly 15% higher risk of later disc degeneration, mostly in the lower back.

Why it matters

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is a common result of aging: blood stem cells pick up mutations and expand into large clones. It is increasingly seen as a source of whole-body inflammation and has been tied to heart disease and other age-related conditions. Back and neck pain from worn spinal discs is one of the most common problems of aging, and inflammation is thought to play a part. This study asks whether somatic mutations in blood cells might be one of the hidden contributors to disc degeneration.

What they did

The authors used data from 258,566 UK Biobank participants who had exome sequencing (reading the protein-coding parts of their genes), which can detect CHIP, and complete information on other health factors. They followed them over time for new diagnoses of intervertebral disc degeneration, identified from coded clinical records, and looked separately at the neck (cervical) and lower back (lumbar). Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted to three different degrees, estimated risk. They also ran exploratory analyses for individual CHIP genes and tested whether blood markers of inflammation might explain part of the link.

What they found

8,113 participants (3.14%) carried CHIP. After full adjustment, any CHIP, defined as a mutant clone making up at least 2% of blood cells, was linked to a higher risk of new disc degeneration (hazard ratio 1.153, 95% CI 1.030 to 1.300). Larger clones of at least 10% carried a similar increase (hazard ratio 1.162, 95% CI 1.010 to 1.340). Exploratory gene-level analyses suggested that mutations in SRSF2, STAG2, GNAS, IDH2 and BRAF may be tied to higher risk. Inflammation markers, namely the systemic immune-inflammation index, white cell count and neutrophil count, appeared to explain only a small share of the link, between 2.15% and 4.41%. Lumbar disc degeneration showed a similar association, but the association for the neck was not statistically significant.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the article is open access. The study is observational, so it shows an association, not proof that CHIP causes disc damage, and unmeasured factors could play a role. The increase in risk is modest. Disc degeneration was taken from diagnosis codes, which miss people who never sought care and do not capture severity. UK Biobank volunteers tend to be healthier than the general population. The gene-level results are exploratory and based on small numbers of carriers, and the inflammation markers explain little of the link.

Key terms

CHIP
An age-related expansion of blood cells descended from one stem cell with a mutation.
Variant allele fraction (VAF)
The share of DNA reads carrying the mutation; a rough measure of clone size.
Intervertebral disc degeneration
Wear and breakdown of the cushioning discs between the bones of the spine.
Hazard ratio
How much more often an outcome occurs over time in one group than another; 1.15 means about 15% more.
Exome sequencing
Reading the protein-coding parts of all genes.
Mediation analysis
A statistical test of how much of a link runs through a third factor.
