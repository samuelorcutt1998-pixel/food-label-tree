When genomes burn: how DNA damage ignites inflammation and fuels ageing

Emmanouil Theodorakis, George A Garinis

Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B · 12 Nov 2026 · Review

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1098/rstb.2025.0337

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A review arguing that DNA damage and chronic inflammation are two sides of one self-reinforcing loop, and that aging is what happens when short-term repair and defence alarms never switch off.

Why it matters

Two of the best-known hallmarks of aging are genome instability (DNA damage that builds up and is repaired less well) and 'inflammaging', the low-grade inflammation common in older people. They are often studied separately. This review, from a group that works on DNA repair and aging in mice, argues they are mechanistically inseparable. If that is right, treatments aimed at only one side may fall short, and breaking the link between them could be a more effective way to protect tissues as we age.

What they did

This is a review and synthesis, not a new experiment. The authors pull together evidence from research on genome maintenance, innate immunity and metabolism. They draw on human syndromes caused by faulty DNA repair, which often look like accelerated aging, and on genetically engineered mice with repair defects. They use this to build a single framework and then discuss its evolutionary meaning, its relevance to disease, and possible therapies. The article is part of a themed issue on 'Developmental circuits in ageing'.

What they found

The review describes how persistent DNA damage, faulty repair and 'transcriptional stress' (damage that gets in the way of reading genes) switch on inflammatory signalling. This happens both through the standard DNA damage response and through innate immune sensors that detect the cell's own DNA or RNA in the wrong place, for example outside the nucleus. Inflammation then feeds back: inflammatory signals, metabolic stress and reactive oxygen and nitrogen molecules cause fresh DNA damage and weaken repair, closing a self-reinforcing loop. Evidence from repair-deficient humans and mice suggests that chronic activation of the damage response reshapes metabolism, hormone signalling, mitochondrial function and immune surveillance well before tissues visibly break down. The authors highlight newer findings on damage-response activity outside the nucleus, RNA-based danger signals and organelle dysfunction as amplifiers of this loop. Their central proposal is that aging emerges when stress circuits that evolved for brief repair and defence keep running chronically. They also discuss therapies meant to interrupt the damage and inflammation axis.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the article is open access. As a review it proposes a framework rather than testing it, and the abstract does not say how studies were chosen, so the selection may favour supporting evidence. Much of the evidence comes from rare repair-defect syndromes and engineered mice, which may exaggerate how important this loop is in normal human aging. The abstract does not name specific therapies or report results from treating people.

Key terms

Genome instability
A tendency for DNA damage and mutations to build up in cells.
Inflammaging
Chronic, low-grade inflammation that rises with age.
DNA damage response (DDR)
The cell's network for sensing DNA damage, pausing growth and calling in repair.
Innate immune sensing
Built-in detectors that sound an alarm when DNA or RNA turns up in the wrong place.
Transcriptional stress
Damage that blocks the machinery that reads genes.
Reactive oxygen species
Unstable oxygen-containing molecules that can damage DNA.
