Early adversity, poverty, and positive social development show distinct associations across epigenetic aging measures in childhood

Libera Ylenia Mastromatteo, Jonas G. Miller

Development and Psychopathology · 9 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1017/s0954579426102132

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

In 863 nine-year-olds, adversity and poverty pushed different epigenetic clocks in different directions, and falling social skills were linked to an older disease-risk clock.

Why it matters

Hard early lives, including adversity and poverty, are thought to leave marks on the body that raise disease risk decades later. Epigenetic clocks, which read biological age from DNA methylation, have been used to look for those marks in children, but results have been inconsistent. One reason may be that different clocks measure different things: some track normal development and maturation, while others were built to predict illness and death. This study compares many clocks side by side in the same children, and it also asks whether something positive, a child's social development, plays a part.

What they did

The authors used data from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study, a large US cohort, with DNA methylation data for 863 children (about half boys, and 46% non-Hispanic Black). At about age 9, they calculated several types of epigenetic clocks: age-trained clocks (Horvath, Hannum, Skin and Blood, and the pediatric PedBE clock), health-related clocks (PhenoAge and GrimAge) and a pace-of-aging measure (DunedinPACE). They related these to early adversity, poverty, and how children's social skills changed across childhood.

What they found

Early adversity was linked to older readings on GrimAge and PedBE, but younger readings on the Horvath and Skin and Blood clocks, so the direction depended on the clock. Poverty showed more consistent links with older readings on the clocks tied to mortality risk. Children whose social skills declined across childhood had higher GrimAge, and this pattern of social development partly explained the link between poverty and GrimAge. The effect was specific to social skills and was not seen for symptoms of psychological problems. The authors conclude that adversity and poverty affect clocks that track maturation differently from clocks that track disease risk.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the article is open access, but its full text was not used here. The reported effect sizes were small (standardized coefficients of roughly 0.1 to 0.2). Clocks were measured at one age, so the study cannot show how epigenetic aging changes over time or that adversity causes the differences. Most adult-derived clocks were not designed for children, which may help explain the mixed directions. The mediation result is statistical and does not prove that improving social skills would change a child's biological aging.

Key terms

Epigenetic clock
A model that estimates biological age from patterns of DNA methylation.
GrimAge
An epigenetic clock built to predict mortality risk.
PedBE
An epigenetic clock designed for children, developed from cheek-cell samples.
DunedinPACE
A DNA methylation measure of how fast a person is aging, rather than how old they are.
Mediation
A statistical test of whether one factor partly explains the link between two others.
