Loss of cell-identity DNA methylation tracks division history, not DNA damage

Naor Sagy, Nadav Benedek, Daniel Zvi Bar

bioRxiv (preprint) · 3 Nov 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.64898/2026.11.03.763914

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A direct test of the idea that DNA damage ages cells by erasing their epigenetic identity finds that repeated cell division, not radiation damage, wears that identity down in human skin cells.

Why it matters

Every cell in the body carries the same DNA, but a skin cell and a liver cell use it differently. Part of what keeps each cell type distinct is DNA methylation, small chemical tags on DNA that help set which genes stay on or off. One influential theory of aging says that DNA damage, and the repair work it triggers, slowly scrambles these tags so that cells lose the information that tells them what they are. If that were true, protecting DNA would also protect cell identity. This preprint sets out to test that specific proposed cause directly, while making clear it is not testing the broader idea that aging involves some loss of epigenetic information.

What they did

According to the abstract, the team built a panel of 9,150 CpG sites (places in DNA that can carry a methylation tag) where human skin fibroblasts differ most from twenty other primary cell types, since those are the sites where identity should be easiest to measure. They noticed that many of these sites mainly record how much a cell has divided, so they separated that layer from true identity marks. They then scored each treatment as the share of identity information lost beyond matched background sites. In 24 matched fibroblast cultures they compared replicative exhaustion (cells dividing until they stop), activation of the cancer gene RAS, a senescence-inducing dose of X-rays, and senescence caused by the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin.

What they found

Most of the panel behaved as a record of proliferation rather than identity, matching the gradual loss of methylation in late-replicating DNA that builds up when cells are grown in culture. Replicative exhaustion cost 24.3 percent of the identity information and RAS activation cost 5.4 percent. A senescence-inducing dose of X-rays cost nothing, at minus 0.5 percent, with identity sites if anything moving slightly further away from other cell types. The authors say their design could have detected a loss as small as 0.4 percent, so they treat this as a real negative result rather than a lack of power. Senescence induced by doxorubicin or by irradiation did not shift the panel either, and cells that kept dividing after irradiation moved further from other cell types rather than collapsing toward them. Their conclusion is that, in human fibroblasts, loss of identity information follows prolonged division, not acute DNA damage.

Limits

This is a preprint and has not yet been through peer review. The work uses one cell type, skin fibroblasts, grown in the lab, and tests acute damage from X-rays and a drug rather than the slow, lifelong damage that happens in a living body, so it may not settle what happens in aging tissues. The identity measure is the authors' own method. This synopsis was written from the abstract only, although the full preprint is free to read.

Key terms

DNA methylation
Small chemical tags on DNA that help control which genes are active, without changing the DNA sequence.
CpG site
A spot in DNA where the letter C is followed by G; these are the main places methylation tags are added.
Fibroblast
A common connective-tissue cell, often grown in the lab from skin samples.
Replicative exhaustion
The point at which cultured cells have divided so many times that they permanently stop.
Senescence
A state in which a cell stops dividing for good but stays alive and active.
Epigenetic information theory of aging
The idea that aging is driven by cells gradually losing the epigenetic marks that define their type and function.
