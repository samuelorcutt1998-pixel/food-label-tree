Environmental Chemical Exposures and Accelerated Epigenetic Aging: Links to Metabolic and Age-Related Health Outcomes

Hyeongyu Shin et al.

medRxiv (preprint) · 12 Nov 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.64898/2026.11.10.26367792

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

In 666 older Korean adults, higher exposure to certain combustion by-products, cadmium and a solvent marker went with a faster GrimAge epigenetic clock, which in turn tracked with a liver enzyme.

Why it matters

Epigenetic clocks estimate biological age from methyl tags on DNA, and GrimAge is one of the versions most strongly linked to disease and death. If everyday chemical exposures speed up these clocks, that would point to a concrete pathway by which pollution could accelerate aging, and to exposures people might reduce. Most earlier work looked at single chemicals; real life involves mixtures.

What they did

The researchers studied 666 older adults in the FROM cohort in South Korea, which focuses on people living in environmentally vulnerable areas. They measured 13 exposures, including heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs, by-products of burning fuel, tobacco and food) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and calculated several epigenetic aging measures from blood. They tested each exposure separately, then the mixture using quantile g-computation and Bayesian kernel machine regression, two methods for chemical mixtures. They linked clock acceleration to clinical blood traits, used two-sample Mendelian randomization (a genetics-based method to probe cause and effect) and ran mediation and exploratory disease analyses.

What they found

GrimAge acceleration showed the clearest associations. Four exposures were significantly linked to faster GrimAge: the PAH breakdown products 2-hydroxyfluorene and 2-naphthol, blood cadmium, and methylhippuric acid (a marker of xylene, a solvent). The strongest was 2-hydroxyfluorene, at about 0.49 years of GrimAge acceleration per doubling of exposure. The combined mixture was also linked to faster GrimAge (about 0.50, with a confidence interval running from 0.01 to 0.98), with the two PAH markers as the main contributors. Among clinical traits, white blood cell count and the liver enzyme GGT rose with GrimAge acceleration, and Mendelian randomization gave nominal support for GrimAge acceleration raising GGT. Part of the link between the four exposures and GGT ran through GrimAge acceleration. Exploratory analyses suggested possible links with diabetic kidney disease and osteoporosis, and an inverse link with heart valve disease.

Limits

This is a preprint that has not been peer reviewed, and this synopsis is based on the abstract only. The study is observational and, as far as the abstract shows, measured at one time point, so it shows associations, not proof that the chemicals cause faster aging. The mixture result only just reached significance. The Mendelian randomization support was described as nominal, and the disease analyses were exploratory. The cohort is from specific Korean communities and may not generalise.

Key terms

GrimAge
An epigenetic clock built to predict lifespan and disease risk from DNA methylation.
Age acceleration
How much older a clock says someone is compared with their actual age.
PAHs
Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, chemicals formed when fuel, tobacco or food is burned.
Quantile g-computation
A statistical method for estimating the combined effect of a chemical mixture.
Mendelian randomization
Using genetic variants as natural experiments to probe cause and effect.
GGT
Gamma-glutamyl transferase, a liver enzyme measured in blood tests.
