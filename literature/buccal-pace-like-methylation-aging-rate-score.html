A Pseudo-Longitudinal Methylome Projection Framework Defines a Buccal PACE-like Projection Index from Cross-Sectional DNA Methylation Data

Tatsuma Shoji, Ryo Nakaki

Computational and Structural Biotechnology Journal · 19 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.34133/csbj.0304

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Researchers built a cheek-swab DNA methylation score meant to estimate the pace of aging without long-term data; it was predictable from swabs but only modestly matched the blood-based DunedinPACE clock.

Why it matters

Most epigenetic clocks estimate how old your body looks at one moment. DunedinPACE is different: it was trained on people followed for decades and estimates how fast you are aging right now, which makes it popular in longevity research and consumer tests. But it was built for blood, and the long-term data needed to build similar pace measures for other tissues usually do not exist. Cheek (buccal) swabs are painless and easy to collect at home, so a reliable buccal pace-of-aging score would be useful. This paper tries to build one from data collected at a single time point.

What they did

The authors used an existing buccal biological age estimator as a stand-in for time. For each methylation site (CpG) they fitted smooth curves describing how methylation shifts with biological age, using cross-validation. They then used the slope of those curves to project each person's cheek methylation pattern a small step forward in time, recalculated biological age from the projected pattern, and treated the change in biological age per unit of time as a pseudo-aging velocity. This was converted into a score centred on 1.0, like DunedinPACE. They then trained models to predict the score directly from buccal methylation, checked the results under different settings, and compared the score with measured blood DunedinPACE. They also tried to rebuild DunedinPACE itself from cheek samples.

What they found

In 151 people, the new score could be predicted reliably from cheek methylation in samples held out from training, with correlations of about 0.71 between observed and predicted scores. Results held across different numbers of CpG sites and different model types. However, the buccal score was only modestly associated with measured blood DunedinPACE, and attempts to reconstruct DunedinPACE from cheek samples, either by training a proxy model or by imputing blood CpG values from buccal ones, performed poorly for individual people. The authors conclude that a tissue-specific pace-like score can be derived from single-time-point cheek data, but stress that it is an exploratory index and not a replacement for blood DunedinPACE.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only. The article is listed as open access, but its full text was not reviewed for this page, so methods details beyond what the abstract states are not given here. The score is predicted well partly because it is derived from the same methylation data and an existing age estimator, so good internal prediction does not show it tracks real health outcomes; the abstract reports no links to disease, function or mortality. The sample of 151 people is small, and the weak match with DunedinPACE means the two scores should not be treated as interchangeable. The method assumes cross-sectional differences between people mirror changes within a person over time, which may not hold.

Key terms

DunedinPACE
A blood DNA methylation measure of the current pace of biological aging, trained on decades of follow-up.
Buccal
Relating to the inner cheek; buccal swabs collect cheek cells.
CpG site
A spot in DNA where a C is followed by a G, the main place methyl tags are added.
Cross-sectional data
Data collected at a single time point from many people, rather than following them over time.
Out-of-fold prediction
Testing a model on samples it was not trained on, to check it generalises.
