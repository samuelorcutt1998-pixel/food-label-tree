Evolution of the human methylome in blood from embryo to ageing and cancer

Jyoti Nangalia et al.

Research Square (preprint) · 4 Nov 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-11370327/v1

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Single-cell blood lineages show that heritable DNA methylation changes far outpace mutations across life, and that epigenetic clocks largely report which clones dominate blood rather than calendar age.

Why it matters

DNA methylation tags help lock in what a cell is, and they are copied when the cell divides. Those tags also drift with age and shift in cancer, but until now it was hard to watch how the methylome evolves inside single human blood lineages over a lifetime. Knowing whether age-related methylation is mostly developmental leftover, clone expansion, or new damage would change how we read epigenetic clocks and clonal hematopoiesis.

What they did

According to the abstract, the team matched whole-methylome and whole-genome sequencing from 1,183 single-cell-derived haematopoietic colonies from 13 people. The donors spanned 17 weeks after conception to 77 years of age, healthy blood formation to malignancy, and included monozygotic twins. That design let them reconstruct when methylation states arose, how often they changed relative to DNA letter mutations, and how those states behaved in clonal expansions and blood cancers.

What they found

Heritable methylation change ran about six orders of magnitude faster than single-base DNA mutations before birth and about four orders faster afterward, often flipping whole stretches of CpG sites at once. Each blood cell already carried about 300,000 somatic methylation changes before birth, including more than 3,000 large segments that then stayed stable for decades. The authors call these large segments embryonic epialleles: places where lineages of the same cell type in one person keep different heritable methylation states. They found about 250,000 such segments across the cohort, many shared between people, and used the mosaic to time X-chromosome inactivation to the first few divisions of life. Epigenetic age, they argue, mainly reports the division history of whichever clones currently dominate the blood, not the person's calendar age. In clonal expansions and blood cancer, many methylation differences versus background were embryonic states the founder cell already carried; expansions nonetheless converged on Polycomb-associated regulatory regions, usually gaining methylation but losing it when DNMT3A was mutated. A few changes at known cancer genes, including CDKN2B, ABL1 in BCR::ABL1 disease, and CACNA1D, arose only inside expansions and looked like epigenetic drivers. Because methylation can change which transcript a gene makes or whether it is expressed, the authors cast methylation mosaicism as another widespread substrate for Darwinian evolution inside the body.

Limits

This is a preprint and has not yet been through peer review. The synopsis was written from the abstract only, although the preprint is free to read. The work is in blood-forming cells, so tissue-specific aging elsewhere is not mapped here. Colony-based sequencing captures heritable states that survive cloning in culture and may miss some unstable marks.

Key terms

DNA methylation
Chemical tags on DNA, usually at CpG sites, that help control gene activity without changing the DNA letters.
Methylome
The genome-wide pattern of DNA methylation in a cell or sample.
Haematopoietic colony
A clone of blood-forming cells grown from a single cell so that cell's genome and methylome can be read.
Embryonic epiallele
A large stretch of DNA where sister blood lineages keep different heritable methylation states from early development.
Epigenetic age
An estimate of biological age from DNA methylation patterns at many sites.
Clonal hematopoiesis
When one blood-forming stem cell's descendants take over a large share of the blood.
DNMT3A
An enzyme that adds new DNA methylation; often mutated in clonal blood expansions and leukemia.
