Mechanical Unloading Disrupts MACF1-Mediated Telomere Homeostasis to Drive Osteocyte Senescence and Bone Loss

Zhihao Chen et al.

Advanced Science · 26 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1002/advs.79017

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Taking load off bone, as in spaceflight or bed rest, shortened telomeres in bone cells through a MACF1-to-telomerase pathway and pushed them into senescence, offering a telomere-based explanation for disuse bone loss.

Why it matters

Astronauts in space and people stuck in bed for long periods lose bone quickly. That loss looks a lot like age-related osteoporosis, which suggests that removing the normal mechanical load on bones may speed up skeletal ageing through a shared mechanism. Osteocytes, the cells buried inside bone, sense physical forces and help control how bone is built up and broken down. If these cells age early, bone may suffer. It was not known how lack of load causes osteocytes to become senescent, meaning they stop dividing and change their behaviour. This study looks for that link, focusing on telomeres, the protective caps at the ends of chromosomes. Telomeres shorten over time, and when they get too short a cell treats them as damaged DNA and can enter senescence, so they are a natural place to look.

What they did

The researchers studied osteocytes under mechanical unloading, the removal of normal physical load. They focused on MACF1, a large protein that links the cell's internal scaffolding and responds to mechanical signals, and traced a chain of steps from MACF1 to telomerase, the enzyme that can lengthen telomeres. They then tested a bone-targeted gene therapy, AAV-DSS-Macf1, which delivers the MACF1 gene to bone, to see whether restoring it would prevent bone loss. The abstract does not describe the exact animal or cell models or how many were used.

What they found

Unloading caused osteocytes to become senescent quickly, and this came with telomere shortening. The driver was a drop in MACF1. Normally MACF1 helps a signalling protein called beta-catenin move into the nucleus, where it switches on a gene regulator called TCF7. TCF7 then turns on TERT, the core part of telomerase, which keeps telomeres long. Unloading broke this MACF1, beta-catenin, TCF7, TERT chain, leading to shorter telomeres and early osteocyte senescence. Restoring MACF1 with the bone-targeted gene therapy repaired the chain and prevented bone loss. The authors conclude that osteocyte senescence may be a cause of disuse osteoporosis and that MACF1 could be a target for protecting bone in space and on Earth.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the full text was not read. The work appears to be in laboratory and animal models; the abstract does not report results in people. Unloading bone loss and normal age-related osteoporosis share features, but they are not the same condition, so the findings may not transfer directly to ordinary ageing. The gene therapy is experimental, and the abstract gives no safety data. Turning up telomerase is a delicate idea because telomerase activity is also used by many cancers. It also remains to be shown whether exercise or other loading in people acts on the same pathway.

Key terms

Osteocyte
A bone cell embedded in the bone matrix that senses mechanical load and helps control bone remodelling.
Mechanical unloading
Removal of the normal physical forces on bone, as in spaceflight or bed rest.
Telomere
Repeated DNA at each chromosome end that protects it and shortens as cells divide.
TERT / telomerase
The enzyme that adds DNA back onto telomeres; TERT is its key protein part.
Cellular senescence
A state in which damaged cells stop dividing but stay active, often releasing inflammatory signals.
