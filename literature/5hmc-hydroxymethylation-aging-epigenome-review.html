Beyond 5mC: 5hmC in the aging epigenome

Anna Reale, Dario Di Bari, Katsiaryna Karpach, Giuseppe Zardo, Angiolina Catizone, Michele Zampieri

Experimental Gerontology · 31 Oct 2026 · Review

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1016/j.exger.2026.113374

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Epigenetic clocks read standard DNA methylation, but a related mark, 5hmC, also shifts with age in its own way and might explain what clocks are really measuring.

Why it matters

Epigenetic clocks estimate biological age from DNA methylation, mostly the mark called 5-methylcytosine (5mC). They are among the most promising measures of how fast someone is aging, yet nobody fully understands what drives the methylation changes they track. One overlooked piece is that 5mC can be chemically oxidised by TET enzymes into other forms, chiefly 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), as well as 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine. These forms were long seen just as steps on the way to removing methylation, but they may carry their own signals. Standard methylation tests often cannot tell 5mC and 5hmC apart, which matters for interpreting clocks.

What they did

This is a review article. According to the abstract, the authors gather current evidence on these oxidised forms of 5mC in mammalian aging, with most attention on 5hmC. They look at how overall 5hmC levels and their spread across the genome change with age, which parts of the genome are affected, how 5hmC changes compare with 5mC changes, and what roles 5hmC might play in cells and tissues.

What they found

The review reports that aging is linked to changes in overall 5hmC levels and in its genome-wide pattern, and that these changes depend on context, such as tissue type. Regions whose 5hmC changes with age turn up in genes, regulatory elements, repetitive sequences and transposable elements (jumping genes). 5hmC and 5mC changes often occur in different places in the genome, which the authors read as a sign that they are partly independent processes. They suggest 5hmC may help control how stable gene activity is, cell identity, inflammation and how vulnerable tissues are. They argue that understanding the TET and 5hmC pathway could improve how epigenetic aging is interpreted and could support clocks built on 5hmC and strategies for healthy aging.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the article is listed as open access. As a review, it summarises other studies and does not present new data; the abstract does not say how studies were chosen. The described changes are context-dependent, so general statements about 5hmC and aging may not hold in every tissue. 5hmC-based clocks are proposed here, not shown to work better than existing ones.

Key terms

5mC
5-methylcytosine, the standard DNA methylation mark read by most epigenetic clocks.
5hmC
5-hydroxymethylcytosine, an oxidised form of 5mC made by TET enzymes.
TET enzymes
Enzymes that oxidise 5mC, a step that can lead to removal of methylation.
Epigenetic clock
A formula that estimates age from DNA methylation levels at selected sites.
Transposable element
A stretch of DNA that can copy or move itself to new places in the genome.
Differentially hydroxymethylated region
A stretch of DNA whose 5hmC level differs between groups, such as young and old.
