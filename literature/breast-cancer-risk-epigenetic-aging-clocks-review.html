Breast cancer risk, epigenetic aging and DNA methylation clocks

Khalid B. Mir, Denys Rujchanarong, Patricia A. Thompson, Christopher J. McCabe, Jeanne S. Mandelblatt, Moray J. Campbell

Clinical Epigenetics · 5 Sep 2026 · Review

← Back to the Longevity course

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A review argues that DNA-methylation clocks capture breast cancer risk beyond calendar age, since known risk factors like oestrogen exposure, BMI and breast density all appear to speed up epigenetic ageing.

Why it matters

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women in the United States and a major cause of cancer death worldwide. Age is a risk factor for every cancer, but people of the same age carry very different risks. Biological age, meaning how worn the body is after years of exposures and lifestyle, has been proposed as one way to explain that spread. Epigenetic clocks, which estimate age from DNA methylation patterns across the genome, offer a measurable stand-in for biological age. This review asks what these clocks can tell us about breast cancer risk, tumour biology and outcomes.

What they did

The authors review how DNA methylation clocks were built and the biology behind them, then gather evidence on links between epigenetic age and breast cancer. They cover risk factors, tumour biology and clinical outcomes, as well as possible uses in early detection, risk grouping and treatment. They also discuss pregnancy, cell-free DNA in the blood, and drugs that act on the epigenome. As a narrative review, it summarises existing studies rather than producing new data. Breast cancer is not one disease: it has several molecular subtypes with different genomic and epigenomic profiles, and the review notes that these subtypes differ in how they start, progress and respond to treatment.

What they found

The review reports that established breast cancer risk factors, including oestrogen exposure, menopause, higher body mass index and dense breast tissue on mammograms, are associated with faster epigenetic ageing. The authors read this as a sign that clocks hold cancer risk information beyond chronological age alone. They describe pregnancy as causing a temporary rise in epigenetic age followed by a possible reversal after birth. They discuss two emerging tools: analysing the size and pattern of cell-free DNA fragments in blood (fragmentomics), and epidrugs that block enzymes such as DNA methyltransferases and histone deacetylases. They conclude that combining methylation-based biological age with other molecular data could help set screening intensity for high-risk women and track whether prevention or survivorship treatments are working.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the full text was not read. It is a narrative review, so it reflects the authors' selection and reading of the literature rather than a systematic pooling of results. Most links between epigenetic age and breast cancer risk come from observational studies, which show association, not cause. The clinical uses discussed are proposals; the abstract does not report any clock that is already validated for routine breast cancer screening. Different clocks can also give different answers for the same person, which complicates clinical use.

Key terms

Epigenetic clock
A formula that estimates biological age from DNA methylation patterns.
DNA methylation (DNAm)
Chemical tags on DNA that help control gene activity and shift with age.
Biological age
An estimate of how aged the body is, as opposed to years since birth.
Cell-free DNA fragmentomics
Study of the size and pattern of DNA fragments in blood, used to detect disease.
Epidrugs
Drugs that change epigenetic marks, such as DNMT or HDAC inhibitors.
