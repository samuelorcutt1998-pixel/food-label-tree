p53 supports healthy aging

Marcos G. Teneche et al.

bioRxiv (preprint) · 17 Nov 2026 · Preprint

Preprint: removing the 'genome guardian' p53 from old mouse livers raised DNA damage and inflammation, while a drug that boosts p53 reduced inflammation and delayed frailty in aged mice.

Why it matters

p53 is often called the guardian of the genome. When DNA is damaged or a cell's copying machinery stalls, p53 can pause the cell, help with repair, push it into senescence or trigger its death. That makes it one of the strongest natural defences against cancer. In ageing research, though, p53 has a mixed reputation. A popular idea, called antagonistic pleiotropy, holds that genes like p53 help us when young (by stopping tumours) but harm us when old, for instance by driving the build-up of inflammatory senescent cells. If that were true, dampening p53 late in life might look attractive. The problem is that this idea had not been properly tested by switching p53 off specifically in old tissue and watching what happens.

What they did

The researchers worked in mouse liver. They first profiled aged livers at the single-cell level, combining RNA, protein, epigenetic and spatial measurements, to see how active the p53 pathway is with age. They then deleted p53 only in hepatocytes (the main liver cells), in either young or aged mice, and compared the consequences. They looked at inflammation, immune-cell infiltration, DNA replication stress, DNA damage, metabolic gene expression and how well the animals coped with a metabolic stress that injures the liver. Finally, they took the opposite approach and gave aged mice HDM201, a small-molecule MDM2 inhibitor that raises p53 activity, and tracked metabolic genes, inflammation and frailty.

What they found

p53 pathway activity rose with age in mouse liver. Deleting p53 from hepatocytes in old mice, but not in young mice, increased inflammation and immune infiltration. Mechanistically, p53 held down DNA replication stress and DNA damage, which are known triggers of inflammatory signalling. In old liver cells p53 also directly bound and maintained the expression of metabolic genes, and old mice without hepatocyte p53 were more vulnerable to liver damage under metabolic stress. Boosting p53 with HDM201 in aged mice reversed the age-related drop in metabolic gene expression, lowered age-related inflammation and delayed the onset of frailty. The authors conclude that, at least in this setting, p53 activity in old tissue is protective rather than harmful, which runs against the antagonistic-pleiotropy prediction.

Limits

This is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed, so its findings may change. This synopsis is based on the abstract only. The work was done in mice and focused on the liver, so it is not known whether the same holds in other organs or in people. The abstract does not give lifespan data, tumour rates under long-term p53 activation, or effect sizes. MDM2 inhibitors are being developed as cancer drugs and can have significant side effects; nothing here shows they are safe or useful for healthy ageing in humans.

Key terms

p53
A protein that senses DNA damage and other stress and decides whether a cell pauses, repairs, becomes senescent or dies.
Antagonistic pleiotropy
The idea that a gene can be beneficial early in life but harmful later.
DNA replication stress
Stalling or slowing of the machinery that copies DNA, which can lead to breaks and damage.
Hepatocyte
The main working cell of the liver.
MDM2 inhibitor
A drug that blocks MDM2, the protein that normally keeps p53 levels low, so p53 activity rises.
Frailty
A measurable decline in strength, resilience and function with age.
