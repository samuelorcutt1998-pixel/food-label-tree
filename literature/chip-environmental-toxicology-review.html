Clonal hematopoiesis as an intermediate endpoint in environmental toxicology: evidence, mechanisms and future applications

Marzia Barbieri, Marta Mollari, Alessandro Ubaldi, Flavia Silvia Galli, Daniele Marcoccia

Biology Direct · 6 Nov 2026 · Review

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

This review treats age-related blood-cell clones with leukemia-linked mutations as a possible middle step between environmental exposures and later disease, and it sorts exposures that may cause mutations from those that may expand clones already present.

Why it matters

As people age, blood-forming stem cells slowly pick up DNA mutations. When a mutation in a leukemia-linked gene gives one stem cell a growth advantage, its descendants can come to make up a noticeable share of the blood even though the person does not have a blood cancer. That state is called clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, or CHIP. It is one of the clearer measurable examples of somatic mutation building up with age, and it has been tied to later blood cancers, heart disease, chronic inflammation, and higher mortality. If environmental exposures help these mutant clones appear or expand, CHIP could serve as an intermediate checkpoint: a sign that an exposure has already changed the blood-forming system, years before frank disease. This review asks what evidence supports that idea.

What they did

This is a review article rather than a new experiment. According to the abstract, the authors examine evidence connecting environmental and occupational exposures to CHIP and discuss mechanisms that could link those exposures to new mutations or to the selective growth of mutant blood stem cells. They also evaluate CHIP as a possible toxicological biomarker and survey newer tools for studying it, including error-corrected sequencing, single-cell genomics, multi-omics approaches, and longitudinal biomonitoring.

What they found

As a review, the paper does not report new laboratory or cohort results of its own. Its main points, as stated in the abstract, are that CHIP is an age-associated expansion of hematopoietic stem cells carrying somatic driver mutations, and that environmental and occupational exposures may contribute to both the emergence and the growth of these clones. The exposures discussed include benzene, pesticides, air pollution, heavy metals, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, or PFAS. Possible routes of influence include DNA damage, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, epigenetic dysregulation, and remodeling of the bone-marrow niche. The authors distinguish contaminants that act mainly as mutagens from those that may instead favor the expansion of mutant clones that are already present. They argue that CHIP is measurable, biologically plausible, and predictive of later disease risk, while also noting limits in how specific it is to any one exposure and how quickly it responds over time.

Limits

This synopsis was written from the abstract only. The article is open access under a CC BY-NC-ND license, but the full text could not be retrieved during this sweep, so how the authors weighed conflicting studies cannot be judged here. Reviews summarize existing work and do not add new measurements, so any claim that a given chemical causes CHIP depends on the quality of the underlying studies. The abstract does not report how strong each exposure–CHIP link is or how well those studies handled confounding by age and smoking. Using CHIP as a marker of a specific environmental insult is also limited by the fact that clones arise with ordinary aging, which the authors themselves flag.

Key terms

Clonal hematopoiesis (CHIP)
An age-related expansion of blood stem cells that carry a leukemia-linked mutation in a person who does not have blood cancer.
Somatic mutation
A DNA change acquired in a body cell during life, not inherited from a parent.
Hematopoietic stem cell
A long-lived bone-marrow cell that gives rise to the many types of blood cells.
Clonal selection
The process by which a cell with a growth advantage out-competes its neighbors and comes to dominate a tissue.
PFAS
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, a large class of persistent industrial chemicals sometimes called forever chemicals.
Error-corrected sequencing
DNA-reading methods designed to catch very rare mutations by reducing the mistakes that ordinary sequencing makes.
