Age-dependent effect of BCG vaccination on the kinetics of biological aging

Cancan Qi et al.

Research Square (preprint) · 26 Nov 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-11451703/v1

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

The BCG vaccine appeared to slow epigenetic ageing in young adults but speed it up in people over 65, a reminder that the same intervention can push DNA-methylation clocks in opposite directions depending on age.

Why it matters

BCG is a century-old tuberculosis vaccine. Beyond tuberculosis, it is known to train the immune system more broadly, and some studies suggest it can boost immune responsiveness and calm long-term, low-grade inflammation. Both of those processes are closely tied to immune ageing, which has made BCG interesting as a possible tool to slow parts of ageing. A common way to measure biological ageing is with epigenetic clocks: formulas that read DNA methylation, the chemical tags on DNA, and estimate how old the body looks at a molecular level. This preprint asks whether BCG vaccination changes epigenetic age, and whether the effect depends on how old the person is when vaccinated.

What they did

The team measured DNA methylation across the genome in two vaccinated groups. The first was 284 young adults from the 300BCG study, sampled before vaccination, two weeks after and three months after. The second was 117 adults over 65 from the BCG-DENMARK-SENIOR study, sampled before vaccination and one year after. They then calculated epigenetic age acceleration, which is how much older or younger someone's methylation looks compared with their real age, plus an inflammation-related age score. They also looked at a possible mechanism involving CD38, a protein on blood stem cells.

What they found

The effects depended on both age and sex. In young people, and especially in young men, BCG vaccination significantly reduced epigenetic age acceleration, and an inflammatory age score fell in parallel. In older adults the opposite happened: vaccination was followed by faster epigenetic ageing. The authors propose that CD38 may help explain this split. According to the abstract, BCG raises CD38 on blood-forming stem cells; in youth this helps the stem cells multiply, but in older organisms it contributes to the stem cells becoming depleted and working less well. The authors argue that BCG's effects should be characterised fully, both as a way to study ageing biology and because of renewed interest in giving vaccines like BCG to older people for their wider immune benefits.

Limits

This is a preprint posted to Research Square and has not been peer reviewed, so its results may change. This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the full text was not read. The two cohorts differ in age, study design and follow-up time (three months versus one year), so the young and old results are not a like-for-like comparison. The abstract does not name the specific clocks used or give effect sizes. The CD38 explanation is described as a possible partial mechanism, not a proven one. A shift in a methylation clock is not the same as a change in health or lifespan, and nothing here should be read as advice for or against BCG vaccination.

Key terms

BCG vaccine
A live, weakened tuberculosis vaccine known to train the immune system more broadly.
Epigenetic clock
A formula that estimates biological age from DNA methylation patterns.
Epigenetic age acceleration
How much older or younger a person's methylation-based age is than their actual age.
Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)
Stem cells in the bone marrow that produce all blood and immune cells.
CD38
A protein on many immune and stem cells, involved in NAD+ breakdown and cell signalling.
