Greater than the sum of its parts: Combining epigenetic clocks to characterize the association of biological age acceleration and adiposity in young Filipino adults

Romaniya S. Voloshchuk, Anthony S. Zannas, Christopher W. Kuzawa, Nanette R. Lee, Delia B. Carba, Linda S. Adair

PLOS One · 25 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0347070

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Merging six epigenetic clocks into one combined score showed that more body fat, especially around the waist, already goes with faster biological ageing in Filipino adults in their early twenties.

Why it matters

Many different epigenetic clocks now exist, each built in a different way and each giving a slightly different answer about how fast someone is ageing. Researchers often have to pick one or report a long list of results that do not fully agree. Excess body fat is known to be linked with faster ageing on several of these clocks, but most of that research comes from older adults in wealthy Western countries. This study tested two things: whether several clocks can be combined into one overall measure, and whether body fat is linked to faster biological ageing in young adults in a lower-middle-income Asian country.

What they did

The researchers analysed 1,745 participants, about 21.7 years old and 45% female, from the Cebu Longitudinal Health and Nutrition Survey in the Philippines. They calculated biological age acceleration with six measures: Horvath, Hannum, PhenoAge, GrimAge, DNAm telomere length and DunedinPACE, each in standard form and in a more noise-resistant principal-component (PC) form. After checking that the data suited the method, they used factor analysis, a statistical way of finding the shared signal in several measures, to build two combined scores, FactorAge and PCFactorAge. Body fat was measured as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio, and each was tested against each ageing measure with linear regression.

What they found

All three fat measures were positively linked to both combined scores. A 5-unit higher BMI went with about a 0.1 standard deviation increase in FactorAge and PCFactorAge. A 10 cm larger waist went with about a 0.09 standard deviation increase in each. A 0.1 higher waist-to-height ratio went with about a 0.16 standard deviation increase in each. Waist-to-height ratio was also linked to higher scores on several individual clocks, including PhenoAge, PCGrimAge and DunedinPACE; waist size and BMI were linked to a smaller set of clocks. The authors conclude that combining clocks is a new way to build an overall biological age estimate, and that fat measures predict it in this young Filipino group.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only. The analysis is cross-sectional, so it cannot show whether extra body fat causes faster epigenetic ageing or the other way round, and other lifestyle factors may play a part. The effect sizes are modest, a tenth to a sixth of a standard deviation, and the combined scores are expressed in standard deviations rather than years, which makes them harder to interpret. The results come from one cohort of young adults and need checking elsewhere. The abstract does not say whether the combined score predicts health outcomes better than single clocks.

Key terms

Epigenetic clock
A formula that estimates age from DNA methylation patterns.
Biological age acceleration
How much faster or slower someone is ageing biologically than their calendar age suggests.
Factor analysis
A statistical method that pulls out the common signal shared by several related measures.
PC clocks
Versions of epigenetic clocks rebuilt from principal components to reduce technical noise.
Waist-to-height ratio
Waist size divided by height; a simple measure of belly fat.
Standard deviation
A measure of spread; here, the unit used for the combined ageing scores.
