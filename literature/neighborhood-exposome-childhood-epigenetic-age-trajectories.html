Neighborhood Exposome at Birth and Trajectories of Epigenetic Age Acceleration Across Childhood

Qingyue E. Yuan et al.

JAMA Network Open · 13 Nov 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.44321

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Following 570 children from age 3 to 13, researchers found that being born into one type of urban neighborhood was linked to a rising epigenetic-age trajectory on one of three clocks.

Why it matters

Epigenetic clocks read chemical tags (methylation) on DNA and estimate a biological age. In adults, a clock age running ahead of real age, called epigenetic age acceleration, has been linked to higher disease risk. Much less is known about how this measure behaves in childhood, and whether the place a child is born into, its social and physical environment, nudges it one way or another. If early surroundings leave a mark on DNA methylation, that would be one route by which neighborhood could shape lifelong health.

What they did

The team used Project Viva, a cohort that enrolled pregnant women in eastern Massachusetts between 1999 and 2002. They studied 570 children who had blood DNA methylation measured at about ages 3, 8 and 13, plus full neighborhood and background data. Each child's birth neighborhood was described with 22 census-tract measures covering four areas: deprivation, social fragmentation, population density and environmental quality. A pattern-finding method (self-organizing maps) grouped these into neighborhood profiles. Epigenetic age acceleration was calculated with three clocks, Horvath, Skin & Blood, and Wu (a clock built for children). Statistical models then sorted children into trajectory groups over time and tested whether neighborhood profile predicted which group a child fell into.

What they found

Three trajectory groups emerged: stable, increasing and decreasing for the Horvath and Skin & Blood clocks, and a low-baseline group in place of the decreasing group for the Wu clock. Three neighborhood profiles were found: suburban with the fewest adverse exposures (278 children), urban with high residential turnover, many single-person households and closeness to highways (168), and urban with high socioeconomic deprivation and pollution (124). Compared with the suburban profile, children in the second profile had higher odds of an increasing Wu-clock trajectory (adjusted odds ratio 1.67, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.69). The deprived, polluted profile showed no association. The authors suggest early neighborhood conditions could be targets for prevention, while saying more work is needed on what this means for later health.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the article is open access but its full text was not reviewed for this summary. The study is observational, so it shows association, not cause. The one positive finding was for a single clock, with a confidence interval close to 1, and it did not appear for the other two clocks or for the most deprived and polluted neighborhoods, so it should be seen as modest and in need of replication. The cohort comes from one region of the United States, and blood methylation in children may not reflect other tissues. Whether a rising childhood clock trajectory predicts adult disease is not shown here.

Key terms

Epigenetic age acceleration
The gap between the age estimated from DNA methylation and a person's actual age, after accounting for age.
Exposome
The total set of environmental exposures a person experiences, here measured at the neighborhood level.
Wu clock
An epigenetic clock developed specifically to estimate age in children.
Self-organizing map
A machine-learning method that groups similar items, here neighborhoods, into profiles.
Odds ratio
How much more likely an outcome is in one group than another; 1.67 means about two-thirds higher odds.
