Elevated baseline DNA damage and hypomethylated circulating free DNA distinguish frailty from healthy aging

Stefania Farina et al.

Cell Death & Disease · 4 Sep 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1038/s41419-026-09207-w

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Frail older people carried more unrepaired DNA damage in their skin cells than healthy people of the same age, and their blood DNA fragments carried a distinct methylation fingerprint, hinting at both a cause and a possible blood test for frailty.

Why it matters

Getting older raises the risk of many diseases, including cancer and brain disorders, and the slow build-up of damage to DNA is thought to be one of the main forces behind this. But people do not all age the same way. Some stay robust into late life, while others become frail: they lose strength and reserve across several body systems at once, become easier to tip into illness, and are more likely to end up disabled. Two people with the same birthday can therefore be in very different shape, and it is not clear what separates frailty from healthy ageing at the molecular level. This study asks whether DNA damage, and traces of DNA released into the blood, look different in frail older people compared with healthy older people of a similar age.

What they did

The researchers compared three groups: frail older individuals, healthy older individuals of a similar age, and healthy young people. From skin samples they grew fibroblasts, the cells that make connective tissue, and measured how much DNA damage these cells carried under normal conditions and how well they responded after being exposed to radiation. They also measured circulating free DNA, the small fragments of DNA released into the bloodstream when cells die or break down, and compared those levels with TNF-alpha, a marker of inflammation. Finally, they sequenced the methylation pattern of the cell-free DNA. Because each tissue has its own methylation signature, this can hint at which tissues the fragments came from, a process called deconvolution. The abstract does not give the number of people in each group.

What they found

Fibroblasts from frail people showed more DNA damage at rest, seen as more spots of two markers of DNA breaks, gamma-H2AX and 53BP1, than cells from age-matched healthy people. However, when the cells were deliberately damaged with radiation, the frail and non-frail groups did not differ in how well they responded. In other words, frail cells seemed to start from a higher damage baseline rather than having an obviously weaker repair response. Frail people also had more cell-free DNA in their blood, and higher levels tracked with higher TNF-alpha, linking the DNA fragments with inflammation. The cell-free DNA from frail people contained many under-methylated fragments, and a set of differently methylated regions could tell frail people apart from age-matched controls. Deconvolution pointed to the small intestine as a possible source of some of this DNA.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only; the full text was not read. The study shows associations at one point in time, so it cannot say whether the extra DNA damage causes frailty or follows from it. The abstract does not report group sizes or how well the methylation fingerprint performed, and the gut-origin finding is described as a possible source that needs confirming. Skin fibroblasts grown in the lab may not reflect every tissue in the body. Any blood test for frailty based on these results would need testing in larger, independent groups first.

Key terms

Frailty
A state of reduced physical reserve in older age across several body systems, raising vulnerability to illness and disability.
Circulating free DNA (cfDNA)
Short fragments of DNA floating in the blood, released when cells die or break down.
gamma-H2AX and 53BP1 foci
Spots inside the nucleus that mark sites of DNA breaks; more spots means more damage.
DNA methylation
Small chemical tags on DNA that help control which genes are used; patterns differ between tissues.
Deconvolution
A computational method that estimates which tissues a mix of DNA came from using their methylation signatures.
